# Critic: Provocateur (Debord) - Round 1 - claims check for scientific accuracy

Anchor used: Debord, The Society of the Spectacle, thesis 1 (the image replacing the thing) and thesis 10 (the spectacle as an affirmation of appearance). Applied here: a mark that looks like rigour, set beside a fact that was never checked. Debord's blind spot (craft, convention) is noted: a legend for numbers is a needed convention, and I do not call the legend itself a spectacle. I attack only where it claims more than it was built to carry.

## Verdict (OBJECT)

The facts underneath are in much better shape than the apparatus on top of them. I found no BLOCKER: every name, symbol, atomic number, date, SI constant and balancer example I could check is right, and the site's "written from general knowledge, not checked" disclaimer is honest and mostly sufficient. The spectacle sits in three places. (a) The headline promise, "every number says how well it is known", is false as written, because the marks cover only the numbers in the "Numbers on this page" blocks and the constants table; the prose is full of unmarked numbers. (b) The three marks are presented as a scale of how well something is known, but they mix three different things (definition, measurement, presentation), so "rounded" can label a better-known quantity than "measured" does, and one mark (the count 118, marked "exact") is a plain misuse. (c) The element classes print "94 metals" as a count, but it includes 15 superheavy elements, among them tennessine and oganesson, which the site's own caption says have mostly predicted chemistry; the detail panel tells the reader "Kind: metal" for a group-18 element. Fix those and one wrong definition of the au, and the claims will be ones the page can back. Separately, the one sentence in about.html that says something was checked ("the balancer is checked against known answers") has no record behind it in the repository. I reproduced the answers myself and they hold, but the page should say who did it and what was tried.

Method and limits: no web access. I read all three data files, build.py, balance-core.js and script.js, and I ran the balancer under node on 10 reactions. Confidence is stated per finding. Nothing was edited.

## Findings

**F1. MAJOR. The headline claim is false as written ("every number says how well it is known").**
What the site says: the `<title>` and og:title of index.html, "Science Encyclopedia: every number says how well it is known"; the hero lede, "A small reference where every number tells you how well it is known"; the meta description, "every number is marked exact, measured or rounded"; the band, "Here every number carries a mark."
What is wrong: marks exist only on the items in `numbers[]` blocks and on the constants table. The prose carries unmarked numbers that matter as much: 1869, 1913, 1866, 1858, 1859, 1912, 1953; "pH 3 is ten times more acidic than pH 4"; "about 1/1836"; "pH 7"; "25 °C"; "eight planets"; "1000 of these"; the 94 / 6 / 18 and 37 counts; and 8 of the 23 topics have no marked number at all (bonds, balancing, redox, genetics, evolution, photosynthesis, water cycle, and also the empty `numbers` of several others). This is the site's whole selling proposition and it is an image of rigour rather than a property. Confidence: high.
Proposed wording: title "Science Encyclopedia: its key numbers say how well they are known". Lede: "A small reference where the key figures are marked: exact by definition, measured, or rounded." Band: "Here the figures listed under each topic and in the constants table carry a mark. Numbers in the running text and the dates do not."

**F2. MAJOR. The three marks are read as one scale of knowledge, but they measure different things, so the ordering the rules imply is wrong.**
What the site says: solid / dashed / dotted rules; blurb for "rounded": "Rounded for readability, or a typical value that varies. Do not use it where precision matters; use the measured figure."
What is wrong: "exact" is a status of a definition; "measured" is a status of an experimental result with a stated uncertainty; "rounded" is a status of the presentation. They are not ranks of knowledge. Concretely, the fine-structure constant is marked "rounded" (dotted) though it is known to about 1.5 parts in 10^10, while G is marked "measured" (dashed) and is known to about 2 parts in 10^5; the dotted mark reads as the weakest and here denotes the stronger knowledge. The age of the Solar System (4.57 billion years, from meteorite dating, uncertainty near a million years) and the electron, proton and neutron masses are measured quantities shown rounded, so they carry the same dotted rule as "typical plate speed" and "typical cell diameter", which are ranges that no one has a more precise figure for. The blurb's "use the measured figure" is therefore impossible for a plate speed. A reader who learns the rule from the homepage demo will draw a wrong conclusion. Also, "measured" is used only on G (1 of 17 constants, 0 of the topic numbers), so the dashed rule is on the page mainly as a legend entry. Confidence: high on the logic and on the examples; medium-high on the uncertainty figures.
Proposed: split the dotted mark into two words, "Rounded" (a measured or computed value shown to fewer digits; see source for more) and "Typical" (a range or an average that varies by case), or at minimum change the blurb to: "Rounded: shown to fewer digits than it is known, or a typical value that varies. Rounded does not mean poorly known; the mass of the electron is known far better than G." Remove "use the measured figure".

**F3. MAJOR. "Known elements 118" is marked EXACT (solid rule) but it is a count, not a definition.**
What the site says: periodic-law, `numbers`: label "Known elements", value "118", mark "exact", note "Atomic numbers 1 to 118, all named."; the exact blurb says "Exact by definition. The number is part of how the unit or constant is defined".
What is wrong: the count is correct today, but it is contingent: a discovery of element 119 would change it. It is exact in the sense that a count is an integer, not exact by definition; it is the one mark on the site that gives a contingent fact the highest-confidence rule. Confidence: high.
Proposed: remove the mark and move to prose: "As of 2026 the table has 118 named elements (atomic numbers 1 to 118)." If a mark is kept, use a new "Counted" wording, never "Exact by definition". Same issue, smaller, for the blurb: "Exact by definition" also stands over conventions (g_n, standard atmosphere, 273.15, au, calorie); change to "Exact by definition or by agreed convention."

**F4. MAJOR. "94 metals" counts 15 superheavy elements whose metallic nature is predicted, and the panel labels group-17 and group-18 members "Kind: metal".**
What the site says: elements.json gives every element 104-118 `"class": "metal"`; chips "Metals 94", "Metalloids 6", "Nonmetals 18"; the home caption "Solid: 94 metals ... Dashed edge: the 15 superheavy elements, whose chemistry is mostly predicted, not measured"; the click-through detail shows "Kind: metal" for Ts (117) and Og (118).
What is wrong: Rf-Cn (104-112) have some measured chemistry and behave as metals, but for 113-118 the metallic character is a prediction (Og is predicted to be a solid semiconductor or possibly nonmetal-like; Ts is predicted to be metalloid-like; Cn is predicted to be unusually volatile). The site's own note says chemistry is mostly predicted, so "metal" is an assignment the site's evidence does not support, yet it is printed as a count (94) and drawn as a solid fill. 94 is one convention's number among several (counts of 91-96 appear in sources). The 6 and 18 are also convention-dependent (At is predicted to be metallic by some sources and is a nonmetal here). Confidence: high that 113-118 are predictions; medium on the exact range of counts other sources print.
Proposed: either set `class` for 113-118 (or at least 117, 118) to a fourth value "unknown" and print "Metals 88 (Rf to Cn included), Metalloids 6, Nonmetals 18, Properties unconfirmed 6", or keep the 94 and add to every place it is printed: "94 metals by this convention, counting the 15 superheavy elements, whose metallic character is predicted rather than observed." Panel line for Og: "Kind: predicted, not measured".

**F5. MAJOR (wrong/outdated definition). The Solar System page defines the astronomical unit from the average Earth-Sun distance, beside an exact figure that says otherwise.**
What the site says: solar-system body, "Distances ... are measured in astronomical units, defined from the average distance between Earth and the Sun." Numbers: "149 597 870 700 m, exact, Fixed by the International Astronomical Union in 2012."
What is wrong: since the IAU 2012 resolution the au is defined as exactly 149 597 870 700 m and no longer depends on the Earth-Sun distance (the old definition was dynamical and tied to the Sun). The page states the old definition in prose and the new one in the number. The two sentences contradict each other. Confidence: high.
Proposed: "Distances inside the Solar System are measured in astronomical units. The unit was originally based on the average distance between Earth and the Sun and is now defined as exactly 149 597 870 700 m."

**F6. MINOR. The balancer sentence in about.html claims a check with no record behind it.**
What the site says: "The equation balancer is checked against known answers, including awkward ones such as the permanganate and hexacyanoferrate reactions."
What I found: no test file, log or list of cases in the repository, and the example chips on balance.html include the permanganate reaction (KMnO4 + HCl) but not a hexacyanoferrate one. By running balance-core.js under node I reproduced the textbook answers: H2+O2 gives 2/1/2; Fe+O2 gives 4/3/2; glucose combustion 1/6/6/6; Ca(OH)2+H3PO4 gives 3/2/1/6; KMnO4+HCl gives 2/16/2/2/5/8; CuSO4 hydrate gives 1/1/5; and K4[Fe(CN)6] + KMnO4 + H2SO4 -> KHSO4 + Fe2(SO4)3 + MnSO4 + HNO3 + CO2 + H2O gives 10/122/299/162/5/122/60/60/188, the well-known answer. Ambiguous input (H2+O2 -> H2O+H2O2) and impossible input (H2 -> O2) return a stated refusal as promised. So the claim is true, but the page cannot show it. Confidence: high on the outputs, medium that these are the exact cases the author meant.
Proposed: "The balancer was run on a short list of textbook reactions, including the permanganate and hexacyanoferrate cases, and gave the published coefficients. The list is not a full test suite." Better: add the hexacyanoferrate reaction to the example chips so the reader can run the claim.

**F7. MINOR. "The exact SI constants and the element names and numbers are the most reliable parts" ranks reliability without a check behind the ranking.**
It is a claim about the author's own work that nothing in the repository supports. In my check both are in fact correct (see list below), but the sentence grades the unverified. Proposed: "The exact SI constants and the element names and numbers are the parts most easily checked against a single published table; the topic prose is a short summary." Confidence: high that it is an ungrounded grade; high that the content passes.

**F8. MINOR. The count of 37 elements without a stable isotope silently excludes bismuth.**
What the site says: "No stable isotope (37)"; "'None' means no stable isotope is known: all 37 are radioactive." Bismuth is shown as "Effectively stable" in the table and the panel only.
What is wrong: strictly, Bi-209 is radioactive (half-life about 2 x 10^19 years), so "no stable isotope" is 38 by the letter. The site's own treatment (Bi flagged separately) is defensible and the 37 is correct under it, but the headline chip and home-page card say 37 without the caveat. Th and U are also long-lived primordial radioactives; the text "all radioactive" is right. Confidence: high.
Proposed, under the table: "37 elements have no stable isotope. Bismuth is counted as stable here because its only isotope decays far more slowly than the age of the universe; strictly it is radioactive, which would make the count 38."

**F9. MINOR. Energy: "in a closed system, its total stays the same."**
Conservation holds for an isolated system; a closed system (no matter exchange) can still take in or lose energy as heat and work. Many physics texts use "closed" loosely, but this site has a thermodynamics entry that uses the strict sense ("isolated system" in the second law). Confidence: medium-high.
Proposed summary: "Energy changes form but, in an isolated system, its total stays the same."

**F10. MINOR. GPS clock wording and mark.**
What the site says: "Net clock offset of a GPS satellite relative to the ground: about 38 µs per day" (rounded); note "About 45 µs fast ... about 7 µs slow."
The figures match the standard values (about +45.7 and -7.2, net about +38.6 µs/day), confidence high. But it is a daily drift rate, not an offset, and the mark "rounded" hides that it is a calculated figure (it depends on the orbit) and that the system corrects for it in the satellite clock design. Also the body says "The satellite navigation system", which is not named; the label names GPS. The home page repeats it under the title "GPS clock offset from relativity".
Proposed label: "Net drift of a GPS satellite clock relative to a ground clock, from relativity: about 38 µs gained per day"; note: "Calculated for a typical GPS orbit: about 45 µs gained from weaker gravity, about 7 µs lost from orbital speed. The system's design corrects for it."; body: "The Global Positioning System relies on both theories".

**F11. MINOR. "Photosynthesis run in reverse" is qualified correctly, but "complementary" hides the energy difference.**
The sentence "It is photosynthesis run in reverse in terms of the overall chemistry" is true for the net equations. The pathways are different (different enzymes, locations, cofactors), the inputs are not interchangeable steps, and the energy goes in as light and comes out as ATP and heat. Confidence: high that the qualification is adequate; medium that a reader will keep it.
Proposed: "The overall equation is the mirror image of photosynthesis's, but the cell uses entirely different steps to get there."

**F12. MINOR. Small oversimplifications, each correctable in one clause.**
- Cell: "Plant cells also have a cell wall and chloroplasts. Animal cells have neither." Fungi and bacteria also have walls, and not every plant cell has chloroplasts (root cells). Proposed: "Plant cells have a cell wall and, in photosynthetic tissue, chloroplasts. Animal cells have neither." Confidence high.
- DNA: "Each strand is a chain of four bases" - a strand is a chain of nucleotides, each carrying one of four bases. Proposed: "Each strand is a chain of units called nucleotides, each carrying one of four bases". Confidence high.
- Periodic law: "Rows (periods) correspond to the filling of successive electron shells." Period lengths (2, 8, 8, 18, 18, 32) come from subshell filling, and 4s fills before 3d. Proposed: "...correspond to the filling of successive groups of electron orbitals." Confidence medium-high.
- pH: summary says "based on its hydrogen ion concentration", body says "activity". Make the summary say "hydrogen ion activity (roughly concentration)". Confidence high. Also "pH 3 is ten times more acidic than pH 4" should read "has ten times the hydrogen ion concentration".
- Human genome "about 3.1 billion": sources give 3.1 to 3.2 billion; proposed "about 3.1 to 3.2 billion". Confidence medium.
- Layout note: IUPAC's 2021 report favours Lu and Lr under Y in group 3. The page says "a common convention" (true) but the about page does not name the alternative's status. Proposed addition: "IUPAC's 2021 recommendation places lutetium and lawrencium in group 3; this page keeps the older layout." Confidence medium (from memory, not verifiable here).

**F13. MINOR (spectacle). Rhetoric about other books on the homepage.**
"Textbooks print 299,792,458 and 6.674 with the same confidence" is an unsupported generalisation about other publications, and false for many (most print 6.674 with a stated uncertainty or "approximately"). Proposed: "A constant that is defined (299 792 458 m/s) and a constant that has to be measured (6.674 x 10^-11) are not the same kind of number, and are often printed as if they were." Confidence: medium.

**F14. MINOR. Constants page lede describes the table as "the defining constants of the SI and a few measured ones".**
The table holds the seven defining constants, five exact conventions (g_n, atm, au, cal, 273.15), one measured constant and four rounded values. "T0, Zero of the Celsius scale" is a convention, not a constant. Proposed lede: "The seven defining constants of the SI, five exact conventions, and five constants known by measurement." Confidence: high.

## Honesty apparatus (item 4)

Sufficient and not overclaiming on the main point. The footer ("A small, readable reference, not a peer-reviewed one. Written from general knowledge and not yet checked line by line against primary sources. For work that matters, use IUPAC, CODATA and NIST.") and the about.html bullet are plain and I found nothing in the content that contradicts them. Two sentences claim more than was checked: the balancer sentence (F6) and the "most reliable parts" ranking (F7). The footer "not yet checked line by line" is also now stale in a good way: I have checked the data and prose line by line from memory (not against primary sources), so the owner may want to say who checked what once the panel is done. One more honest-wording gap: the home page and constants note say "Exact values are fixed by definition of the SI (2019) or by convention" and "The measured value of G is from the CODATA adjustments"; G = 6.674 30(15) is the 2018 value and unchanged in 2022, so the sentence holds, but it names no year. Proposed: "G is the CODATA 2018 and 2022 recommended value" (medium confidence on 2022).

## Where the marks perform a rigour the site has not earned (item 5)

1. Headline and meta (F1): "every number" is the largest overclaim on the site.
2. The solid/dashed/dotted hierarchy (F2): three rule styles read as an ordinal scale of quality; they are three categories.
3. The 118 solid rule (F3).
4. The dotted rule on quantities that are measured to high precision, for example the masses, alpha, 4.57 Gyr (F2).
5. The solid fill of superheavy squares as "metal", with a dashed edge as a partial disclaimer (F4): the edge says "predicted" but the fill says "metal", and the fill is what the eye reads, and what the count of 94 counts.
6. The counts "computed from the data, so the drawing and the text cannot disagree" (build.py docstring): this guarantees internal agreement, not truth. A wrong class assignment prints a wrong count in four places without a flaw in the build. Not on a page, so only a note for the owner.
